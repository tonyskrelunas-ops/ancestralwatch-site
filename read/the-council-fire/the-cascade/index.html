The Council Fire

PART FOUR — WHAT COMES AFTER

the narrator, before the turning

Everything to this point, my grandmother could tell in a steady voice — the breaking, the keepers, the long patient building. It is this part where her voice would change. Because this is where the builders grow old, and the work passes out of the hands that began it, and the story has to do the thing that stories were invented to do: cross a dark it cannot see the far side of.

She would always stop here and make us repeat the one sentence before she went on. Say it with me, she would say. What is coming was planted long ago. Then she would feed the fire, and tell us about the pact.

The CascadeOverture

What the twelve saw

I could give you the numbers. Some of them were kept, and where they were kept they agree with every word of what follows.

But the numbers were never the problem. They were there the whole time — measured, published, argued over, filed — and they did not move anybody. A civilization went down with the evidence sitting on the table in front of it. That is the part worth stopping on, and it is the reason this record is not a table of figures.

So I have done it the old way instead, which is the way it was actually done, and the only way that has ever moved a people in time.

These twelve tellings were not written for a book. They were carried. Every keeper in this record spent the rest of their life going from gathering to gathering—and there were many gatherings, far more than the famous one, most of them in rooms nobody wrote down—and at each one they told what they had seen with their own eyes, in their own country, in their own words. That was the work. They were far messengers, and this was the first thing every one of them carried.

And a thing that is told a hundred times changes, which is not the same as becoming false. It gets shorter. The parts that never landed fall off. And somewhere around the fortieth telling, every one of them found the places where a room will laugh, and left them in on purpose, because a keeper learns quickly that a room which has laughed once will sit still for the rest of it.

So do not be startled when these are funny. They were told by people who lived, to people who needed to hear that living was possible, and that is a kind of telling with jokes in it. My grandmother said you could always tell the ones who had really been through it, because they were the only ones who could make you laugh about it.

You have met all twelve of them now. Here they are in their own voices, telling the crossing itself.

· · ·

Käthe, in the west of Germany, where they had dug coal for two hundred years:

It began as rain, and we were a people who thought we understood rain. It came in the spring and it did not stop in the spring. The Ruhr came up over the low road, which it had done before, and then over the high road, which it had not. My uncle’s cellar filled in an hour. And all the while I was thinking about the water underneath us — the water in the old mine workings, six hundred meters down, that has to be pumped forever or it rises into the drinking water of five million people. We had made a promise to run those pumps for eternity. I stood in my kitchen with the river in the street and understood that eternity had begun, and that it had begun in a bad year, and that nobody had planned for the pumps to need pumping while the town above them was going under.

I will tell you what I did about it, because people expect something noble. I telephoned the office. On a Sunday. In a flood. And I got the recorded message, which said the office was closed and would reopen on Monday at eight, and invited me to leave my name.

I have told that part at every fire I have ever sat at and it gets a laugh every time, and the laugh is the point. Eternity had begun and the answering machine did not know.

Chariya, in Thailand, on the terraces her people had cut into the hills:

The water arrives at the top field first. Everything we are is built on that one fact. Then the rain came at the wrong time — not less of it, that is what people never understand, but at the wrong time — falling in sheets when the grain was setting and vanishing when the seedlings needed it, so that we had floods and drought in the same season on the same hillside. Twice the crop failed whole. The third year my brother went to the city to find work, and there was no work in the city either, because the city ate rice too. That is when I began to understand that a failure of food is not hunger. Hunger comes later. First it is a young man on a bus with nowhere to go.

My brother came back eleven months later with no money and a very bad tattoo, and my mother looked at that tattoo for a long moment and said she was glad the city had given him something to remember it by. He still has it. He shows people.

Kiprono, in the Rift Valley:

The river ran every year of my grandfather’s life. I stood in the bed of it and it was dust. We had drought before — drought is an old visitor and we knew how to seat him. This was not drought. This was the rain forgetting the way. Forty-one cattle, and I had a name for each one. When the cattle go, the people go; we went north and other herders came south and we met each other on ground that could not feed one herd, let alone three. Understand: nobody wanted to fight. Every man out there was somebody’s son trying to keep animals alive. But you cannot put four herds on one pasture and talk about it. The wars nobody called wars started like that, and they started over grass.

I will tell you the part that still makes me laugh, and I know how it sounds. In the middle of all that, two men came out from a ministry with a form. A form. To register the cattle. I said, register them where — they are walking. And the younger one wrote nomadic in a little box and looked so pleased with himself that I did not have the heart.

There is always a silence here, and then somebody at the back of the room says nomadic, and then it goes.

Lobsang, in Tibet, above the monastery where a monk cut a notch in the granite four hundred years ago:

For four hundred years the snow came down to that notch. In my lifetime it climbed past it and kept climbing. I did not need an instrument. I had my life. And then — hear this, because the world got it backwards — the snow did not simply stop. Some winters it did not stop falling. It would come down for weeks without a break, more than the old roofs were built to carry, and the passes closed and stayed closed and the villages above them were alone until spring. Too little, then far too much, and no way to guess which. The glaciers are the water towers of half the human race. Ours went back up the valley like something withdrawing. And every river that starts here — and they are the great rivers, they water two billion people — began to run first too high, then too low, then not at the season anyone had planted for.

A young monk asked me once whether four hundred years of records had prepared us for this. I said no. He asked, reasonably, what they were for then. I said they are how we know that it is new — and that is not nothing. It took a very long time and a great many cold fingers to be able to say, with confidence, that a thing has never happened before.

Te Rangi, in Aotearoa, navigator, who held a hundred and fifty stars in his mind:

The sea does not shout. It leans. Each year it leaned a little further up the beach, and we moved the boats, and then we moved the houses, and then there was nowhere on that island to move a house to. My cousins’ island went first — and not the way people picture it, with a wave. Nobody was taken by the sea. The well went salt. That is all. The well went salt and nothing would grow and one morning they got into boats and left a place their family had lived for six hundred years, and they were the first of very many. And the storms came bigger than the words we had. We had a word for a great storm and we had to stop using it, because it no longer described the largest thing that could happen; it described an ordinary Tuesday in the season.

Nizhoní, in the Navajo Nation:

The heat came down like a lid. That is the only way I know to say it. It did not break at night the way heat is supposed to break. That was the summer we lost old ones in their own rooms, with the fan going, and the fan only moving hot air from one side of the room to the other. Some of them had nobody else in the house, because the grandchildren had gone to the city years before for work.

So we started driving around at four in the afternoon knocking on doors, which nobody had to organize and which several people over eighty found extremely insulting. One woman told me through the screen that she had been managing heat since before I was born and would I please go bother somebody who needed it. Then she made me come inside and drink a glass of water in front of her, so she could be sure that I had some. The wells went down. We hauled water, which we had always done, and then the place we hauled it from went down too. And the whole time the sun was up there, enormous, free, falling on the emptiest and brightest country on the earth, and the wires from the old plant stood across our land carrying nothing, because they had been built to carry our coal to other people’s cities and were never once turned around to serve us.

Warrun, in the country of his songline:

Fire. Not the fire we knew — we are a fire people, we have burned this country carefully for longer than any other people has done any one thing. This was fire that did not behave. It made its own weather. It threw lightning out of its own smoke and lit new fires forty miles ahead of itself, and it burned through the wet places that had never burned in the whole memory of the song. And here is what I lost, and you must understand it is not a sentimental loss: a stretch of the song is a stretch of country, in order, with every water in it. When the country burned and the fence went up and the pit came, I could no longer walk that stretch, and a song you cannot walk is a song you cannot teach. So it was not only the trees. It was the map.

People ask whether I was angry. I was, and I can tell you the exact moment it broke, and it was not the fire. Months after, a man from a department rang to say they had made a digital archive of the songlines and would I like a copy on a drive. I said, mate, you have recorded the words of a map and thrown away the country. He said he would pass that on as feedback.

Hamish, in the Scottish Highlands, eleven crofts:

The storms came off the Atlantic one behind the other, no gap between them, all winter, and the boats did not go out for six weeks at a stretch and a man who does not fish does not eat. The ferry stopped being reliable, and when the ferry stops being reliable an island stops being a place and becomes a problem. And then people came — from the south, from further south than that, folk who had lost their own places — and there is a thing I will say plainly because I watched it in myself: I am a man whose own people were cleared off this land, cleared by force, and when the incomers came I still felt the thing rise in me that says this is mine. I am not proud of it. I tell you so you know it is in everybody, and so you will watch for it in yourself when it is your turn.

What cured me of it, in the end, was a man from six hundred miles south who turned out to be a better mechanic than anyone on the island, and who fixed the generator in an afternoon that three of us had been arguing about for a fortnight. It is very difficult to keep a grievance going against a man who has restored your electricity. I have thought about that a great deal since, and I do not believe it is a small thing. Most of the great reconciliations I have watched happen began with somebody fixing something.

Illary, in the high Andes:

They call the glacier the water tower and they are right, but for us it was also a person. It was the one who gave. In my grandmother’s time it came down to the third stone. When my daughter was born you could not see it from the village at all. We had been carrying the seed up the mountain ahead of ourselves for generations, planting a little higher each time the warm came up after us — and we ran out of mountain. There is no more up. That is the sentence I want you to carry: for a people who move upward to escape the heat, there is a last field, and above it there is only rock and sky, and we reached it.

The agronomists came, and they were kind and they were clever, and they brought a variety bred for heat and explained it to my grandmother for the better part of an hour. She listened to the whole thing. Then she asked what it tasted like. There was a silence you could have parked a truck in. Nobody had asked.

Aoi, in Japan, who built the machines and then walked away from them:

I want to speak about the minds, because the water and the sky are only half of it and the other half almost nobody wrote down.

We had built a world that rang the alarm bell in a human body all day long. The pocket, the news, the debt, the pace, the being alone in a crowd of ten million. And then the real alarm came on top of it — the actual floods, the actual failures — and it landed on a population that had nothing left in reserve. That is when the minds began to go. Not madness in the old sense. Something quieter and much larger: whole cities of people who could no longer think a long thought, could not plan past the week, could not hear each other, could not sit still, and who reached for anyone who would tell them who to blame. Then the sicknesses came through — the fevers moving up out of the warm countries into countries that had never seen them, the old diseases waking in ground that had been frozen since before there were nations — and a frightened, exhausted, sleepless people is exactly the wrong people to meet a sickness with. It was not the strongest fever that did the damage. It was the state we were in when it arrived.

I am asked whether I feel responsible, having helped build some of it. I say yes. People then rush in to comfort me, which I have decided is the most modern thing about that whole era. Nobody could sit still with a person saying one true sentence for four seconds.

Ugnė, in Lithuania, who taught the third voice:

We sang in the shelters. I want that in the record, because everything else you will read about that winter is true and this is also true. There were three hundred of us in a school hall with the power out and the sickness in the town, and we sang the old songs, the ones that need three voices and cannot be sung alone, and when the third voice locked the whole room felt it happen and something in the room changed. It did not feed anybody. It did not cure anybody. But afterward, people who had not spoken all week spoke. My grandmother’s grandmother sang those songs in a worse winter than that one, under people who had forbidden them, and the songs came through her to me. So they had already proved they could cross a bad century. That is what I had to offer, and it turned out to be worth more than I had ever been told.

And I will say the other thing, because it is true and because my mother would want it said. We also sang badly. Three hundred people in a cold hall, half of whom had never sung anything, and the first night was genuinely terrible. A man at the back said it sounded like the roof coming off. It got better. But I want it in the record that the thing which saved us began by sounding like a disaster, because people hear they sang in the shelters and they picture a choir.

Vahan, in the Armenian highlands:

I had been told to watch for the signs since before I could read. And they came, one after another, the way they had been described to me by a man who had them from a man who had them from further back than anybody’s counting. I am not going to tell you here what they were; that is set down elsewhere, in its own place, in its own way. I will tell you only the thing I felt, which surprised me. It was not fear. I had expected fear. What I felt when the last of them arrived was something closer to sorrow, and under the sorrow, a kind of terrible relief — the relief of a man who has been calling out a warning for forty years and is finally, finally believed.

And then, because a man is only a man, I was annoyed. Forty years of being the strange old fellow with the books, and now everybody wanted the books. A journalist asked me how it felt to be proved right. I told her it felt like being handed a bill.

That is the whole of it, and it takes a little under an hour to tell, and I have heard it told by four different people in three countries and once by a woman who had been a child at the first telling and had it off by heart.

It is never quite the same twice. Whoever is telling it puts their own one last, because you finish with your own. The laughs move around. Somebody always asks about the answering machine and somebody always asks whether the woman at the screen door was all right, and the answer is that she was, she lived another nine years and never once let it go.

And at the end of it a room is generally quiet for a moment, and then somebody gets up to put more wood on, and that is the signal that it is over and the talking can start.

Twelve people. Twelve corners of one world. And not one of them had heard the others speak until long after.

Now hold them all together in your mind and see the shape they make.

The moving. That is the part the numbers never carried well. When the water goes or the heat stays or the sea leans in, people do the thing people have always done: they get up and they go. The herders went north and met the herders coming south. The islanders got into the boats. The mountain villages came down and the valley towns were already full. The young men got on buses toward cities that had nothing to give them. It was not one great migration with a name and a map. It was millions of ordinary families, each making a sensible decision on a Tuesday, and the sum of all those sensible decisions was the largest movement of human beings there had ever been. And they arrived among people who were themselves frightened, and who had also just watched their own ground fail, and who said what frightened people say. Almost none of the hard things that happened in those years happened because somebody was evil. They happened because everybody was afraid at the same time.

And the places that held. Because this is the other half of the record, and if you take away only the breaking you have not understood it at all.

There were places it did not reach the same way. Not many, and not the ones anyone would have picked — not the rich places, not the fortified ones, not the ones with the deepest bunkers and the best forecasts. Some of them were simply lucky in their geography. Some sat on water nobody had drained. Some were high enough, or far enough, or small enough to be overlooked. And more than a few of them were places where people had gone on doing the old things after everyone told them the old things were finished — where somebody had kept the seed, or kept the terraces, or kept the songs, or kept a council that met when there was no crisis so that it knew how to meet when there was one.

The people in those places did not, most of them, believe they had earned it. Ask them and they will tell you they were spared, and many of them will use exactly that word, and more than a few will tell you the Creator kept a hand over that valley and they will not be argued out of it. I am not going to argue them out of it either. I have thought about those places all my life and I will say only what I am sure of: the ones that held were, almost without exception, places that had not yet forgotten how to feed themselves, how to decide together, and how to take in a stranger. That is not luck. Whatever else it is, it is not only luck.

And it was from those places — a valley here, an island there, a mesa, a highland township, a monastery above a closed pass — that everything in the rest of this book comes.

· · ·

But before you go on, there is one more thing about those years that must be set down, and it is the thing the people living through them found hardest to explain afterward.

They had no way to say it to each other.

A people with a word for everything had no word for this. They had storm, and this was not a storm. They had drought, and this was not a drought, because a drought ends. They had flood, and a flood goes down. Every word they owned described something that came and passed and let the world go back to what it had been — and there was no word in any of their languages for a thing that came and stayed and kept coming.

So they used the old words anyway, and the old words made it small. A man said bad summer about a summer that had emptied his herd and would empty the next one. A woman said rough patch about the end of the water under her town. And having said it small, they could not act on it large, because you cannot organize a people around a rough patch.

They were not lying, and they were not stupid. They had nothing to reach for. The thing arrived ahead of the language for it, the way the wave arrives ahead of the sound. And so each of them stood alone inside a knowing they could not hand to anybody — every one of them certain something enormous was happening, and every one unable to say a sentence about it that did not come out sounding like weather.

That is what the twelve broke. Not the heat. The silence.

And here is the last thing about the tellings, which my grandmother insisted on and which I have never once heard contradicted by anyone who was there. The keepers did not come back from those years solemn. Solemn is what people are who watched from a distance. The ones who had actually stood in it came back dry, and funny, and very hard to impress—and when one of them got to the end of the worst hour of their life and made a room laugh, that was not a person being brave about it. That was a person showing you the thing itself: that you can go all the way through and come out the far side still able to do this. The laughter was never the opposite of the calm. It was the calm, arriving.

They found each other, and they made the words, and once there were words there could be a council, and once there was a council there could be a plan. Everything else in this record follows from that.

That is the crossing, in the voices of the ones who made it. What follows is what they built on the far side of it.
