The raising

A hogan on Big Mountain, and a kerosene lamp.

No electricity. Four hundred sheep. A ceremony every dawn, before the day was allowed to ask anything of him.

He was raised by his great-grandmother. The day started outside, facing east, with corn pollen and a song — the oldest continuous performance-management system on the continent, and nobody in that camp would have called it that.

He learned to read by kerosene lamp. And by that same lamp he read the philosophy of Japan, and has been apprenticed to that tradition ever since — by choice, not by blood. Three inheritances, then: two that came down the line and one he walked toward.

What the camp taught was not romance. It was method. How to spend nothing you don’t have to. How to hold a state when the weather turns. How to count wealth in four accounts instead of one — people, land, knowledge, and only then money.

Everything in these books came out of that camp first, was tested in a working life second, and is now the subject of doctoral research third. Lived, proven, studied.

The return

He remembered who he was — and his power came back.

College handed him an identity crisis and an inferiority crisis at once — a world that quietly required him to be someone generic, and made him feel smaller for not being it. For a while he tried to be it. He drifted from the teachings he was raised on.

What turned it was a single realisation: he could be who he was. Not a version of himself sanded down to fit the room. He walked back in, and finished at the top of his class.

That is the whole argument of this work. The tools were never lost. They were only set down.

Twelve years · the high desert

He built a Japanese garden where nothing should grow.

A koi pond. A waterfall. A torii with a Buddha beneath it. An arched stone bridge. A meditation room framing a sacred mountain. Then he sold the house, and let it go.

It took twelve years, and it was never a project. It was the Japanese apprenticeship made physical — restraint, repair, and the discipline of doing one thing completely before starting the next. He hauled the stone. He set the bridge. He learned what a waterfall needs at seven thousand feet.

People asked whether it hurt to leave it. It did. It was also the point. A garden in the high desert is a lesson in impermanence you build with your own hands and then hand back — which is the same lesson the camp taught, in another language, on the other side of the world.

The method

A metamodernist, and deliberately a hopeful one.

Refusing both the modern claim that the old knowledge was superstition, and the postmodern claim that no knowledge is truer than another.

The ancestral practices were tested — ten thousand years of field trials with survival as the endpoint. The laboratory findings are real. Neither has to defeat the other. Fiction can carry working instruction. A ceremony can be described by its effect without disclosing its content. A practice can be honoured and examined at once.

That is not a hedge. It is the only position from which this work can honestly be done.

And the through-line of everything he makes is that the tools were never lost — only set down — and that a person, a family, a whole people can pick them back up.

The inheritance is still there. Most of us are richer than we were ever told.
