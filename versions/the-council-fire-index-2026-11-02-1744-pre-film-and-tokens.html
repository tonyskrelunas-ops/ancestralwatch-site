The book

We are entering a time of global uncertainty.

Most of us are living in fight or flight. In urgency. In a noise so constant that we have lost the ability to sit with a story long enough for it to change anything in us.

There is a path through. It has been walked before.

Not as a theory. These same challenges — the weather turning, the work going quiet, the systems that stop answering — were met by our own ancestors, on every continent, in every language, and they came through them still singing. They left the instructions in the body, in the story, and in the song. We did not lose them. We stopped reading them.

Before there was an internet, there was a body that could cross a desert with a library inside it.

For most of human history, everything that mattered moved one way: inside a trained human being. The far messengers of the deserts, who carried what the people needed across the country that kills. The far messengers of the oceans and the waters, who found one island in a sea with no end in sight by reading the swell with their backs. The far messengers of the mountains, who ran the stone roads above the clouds and came down through the failing snows. And the far messengers of time — the ones who carried a people’s whole knowing to a great-grandchild they would never meet.

Every people on earth raised them. Every people called it the highest work there was. Every one of us has them in our line.

Twelve keepers, on six continents, none of whom has heard the others speak. A woman in a German coalfield keeping pumps running that can never be switched off. A seed keeper in the Andes carrying a thousand years of potatoes up a mountain ahead of the heat. A Diné woman who comes home to learn the rest of what her grandmother knew. A Lithuanian song keeper. An Armenian who guards the old books. A monk keeping the record of a failing glacier. A Māori navigator, a Kalenjin orkoiyot, a Scottish crofter, and a Thai rice keeper who has performed every ceremony alone for years.

They find each other. They make the words for what is happening, because the old words made it small. And then they build.

What they build is an ark — not a boat, a method. A way of making certain that what a people knows survives the passage, held in the body, in the story, in the song, and in twelve small objects handed from one pair of hands to the next. Every valley its own fire. Every fire its own keeper. Nothing kept in one place where one bad century can take it.

And on the far side, they rebuild — deliberately, out of what already worked. Wealth counted in four kinds instead of one. An economy built so that it cannot leave town. A way of choosing leaders that does not break the ones who are not chosen. One question asked of everything before it is built: does this honor the great-grandchildren?

Underneath all of it is a single aim, and it is not survival. It is to raise people whole enough to cross — settled in the body, clear in the mind, able to carry what matters through a hard passage and arrive with it undamaged. That is what the healing is for. Not comfort. Capacity.

This is a book about rebuilding two generations at once — ours, and the one that comes after. So that the ones who inherit this are finally healed and genuinely resilient. So that when it is their turn to build, they build from knowledge instead of from panic, and they do not repeat what we are living through now.

The record is written seven generations on, by someone holding all twelve tokens, in a world made out of the broken one. It is hard there, and it is good.

Why it reads the way it does

Why a book about collapse is funny

No people ever crossed hard ground grimly. They crossed it teasing each other, naming the difficult one, laughing hardest in the worst week — because a person who can still laugh is still whole enough to carry the load, and a camp that has lost its humor has already lost. The humor in this book is not relief from the argument. It is the argument.

And a story without Coyote is missing its key teacher. Every people kept one: the trickster who does everything the wrong way around and is holy for it, who gets caught being greedy so that the lesson lands on you before you have finished laughing. The keepers tell those stories in their proper season, and the book is built to be told the same way.

What the dying world forgot, and what every people who came through something has always known: joy is not the reward at the end of survival. It is how the surviving is done.

A longer read

The world it opens in is the one outside your window.

The fires that come earlier every year. The storms we run out of letters to name. The heat that used to be a record and is now a season. And underneath the weather, a quieter worry — the sense that the systems built to hold us have stopped being able to think past the next quarter. The Council Fire begins there. Not in despair. In a circle of people who have watched worlds end before, and remember what a person keeps when the lights go out.

The Council Fire · Part Two — The Keepers

The summer the sky went the color of an old bruise and the news gave up counting the fires, twelve people who had never met sat down around one small fire they had made on purpose.

Outside the ring of their faces, everything was hurrying. Inside it, no one hurried. Each of them had been raised by a people who had already lived through the end of a world — a conquest, a plague, a drowned coast, a march at gunpoint, a language made a crime — and had come out the far side still singing. They carried the one thing the panicking world had mislaid: the knowledge that an ending is not the same as the end, and that what a people does in the first hours of one decides all the hours after.

Gabija spoke first, because someone had to, and hers was a nation that had kept its songs through a century of occupations that made the singing a punishable thing. “My grandmothers were not stronger than the empire,” she said. “They were more patient than it. They sang low, and they sang anyway, and they outlasted it. That is the whole secret. You do not out-shout the fire. You keep the seed the fire cannot reach, and you carry it across to the people on the other side.”

They had not come to save the world; no twelve people can save a world. They had come to make certain that what their peoples had learned the hard way, over ten thousand years, did not go into the fire with everything else…

The Council Fire · Part Three — What They Built

The great towers still marched across the desert — the wires that had carried coal-fired electricity off to light distant cities while the families beneath them lived without power. The wires were empty now. And the desert sat in the dead center of the sun's country.

“They offered us a royalty,” Nizhoní told the council, and her voice went cold and clear the way it did. “The same shape as the coal deal. Let an outside company own the sun off our land, take the wealth down the wire, leave us a check and a hole. I told them no. I told them: the sun is not a thing under the ground that runs out. It comes back every morning, free, forever, on our land. We will not rent it. We will own it.”

The others nodded. Vahan spoke of the wheel of eternity his people had carved into stone for a thousand years. Lobsang spoke of the sun that had always been sacred. Te Rangi spoke of the waves that had carried his grandfathers, and of reading the swell with the body when every instrument had failed. And they all agreed: the energy was theirs, and they would own it.

So they built the thing the dying world could not imagine: power made on the people's land, owned by the people, the wealth circling home — and a way of living that a hundred years of weather could not take…
